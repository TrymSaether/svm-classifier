\section{Experiments and Results}\label{chap:experiments_results}
In this section, we present the experimental validation of our SVM implementations. 
We start with a synthetic linearly separable dataset to compare the primal and dual formulations, followed by experiments with non-linear data using various kernels, and finally evaluate performance on the real-world Breast Cancer Wisconsin dataset\cite{breast_cancer}.

\subsection{Linear Separable Data}
First we generate a linearly separable synthetic dataset with $100$ points in 2D using \texttt{make\_toy\_data} function to create data with a clear margin between the two classes. 
Our optimization problem has the solutions \(\symbf{w}^\star = [1, 1]\), and \(b^\star = 0\) with noise parameter $\sigma = 0.1$ used in data generation.

\subsubsection{Primal SVM}
We trained the Primal SVM with $C=1.0$ using subgradient descent for $10,000$ iterations with a learning rate (lr) of 0.001 and Barzilai-Borwein steps. 
Figure~\ref{fig:primal_svm} shows the decision boundary, margins, and support vectors identified by the model. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{figures/primal_svm.png}
    \caption{Primal SVM with linear kernel ($C=1.0$). 
    The solid line represents the decision boundary, and the dashed lines represent the margins. 
    Support vectors are highlighted with black circles.}
    \label{fig:primal_svm}
\end{figure}

The primal formulation achieved 100\% accuracy on the training data. 
The algorithm correctly identified two support vectors (one from each class), which lie exactly on the margin boundaries. 
The weight vector converged approximately to $\mathbf{w} \approx [0.7069, 0.7073]$, which is just the normalized primal solution. with a bias term $b = -0.001$, resulting in a maximum margin hyperplane.

\subsubsection{Dual SVM with Linear Kernel}
We trained the Dual SVM with the same $C=1.0$ parameter using a linear kernel. 
The dual formulation also achieved 100\% accuracy and identified the same support vectors as the primal approach. Figure~\ref{fig:dual_svm} shows the resulting decision boundary.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{figures/dual_svm.png}
    \caption{Dual SVM with linear kernel ($C=1.0$). The decision boundary and margins are nearly identical to those found by the primal formulation.}
    \label{fig:dual_svm}
\end{figure}

When we recovered the weight vector from the dual solution we again obtained $\mathbf{w} \approx [0.7071, 0.7071]$. 
This confirms the theoretical equivalence of the primal and dual formulations for linear separable data.

\subsubsection{Dual SVM with RBF Kernel}
We trained the Dual SVM with an RBF kernel ($C=1.0$, $\gamma=0.5$) on the same dataset. The model achieved 100\% accuracy, and the decision boundary is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dual_svm_rbf}.
The RBF kernel allows the model to create a non-linear decision boundary, which perfectly separates the two classes with 100\% accuracy. The support vectors are again highlighted, and we observe that they are not necessarily on the margin boundaries as in the linear case.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{figures/dual_svm_rbf.png}
    \caption{Dual SVM with RBF kernel ($C=1.0$, $\gamma=0.5$). The decision boundary is non-linear, effectively separating the two classes.}
    \label{fig:dual_svm_rbf}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Effect of Regularization Parameter}
To understand the impact of the regularization parameter $C$, we trained the primal SVM with three different values: $C = \{0.1, 1.0, 10.0\}$. 
Figure~\ref{fig:c_values} shows how the decision boundaries and margins change with different $C$ values.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/primal_svm_c_values.png}
    \caption{Comparison of different $C$ values in Primal SVM. Lower $C$ values (left) result in wider margins but may allow more training errors, while higher $C$ values (right) enforce stricter classification at the cost of narrower margins.}
    \label{fig:c_values}
\end{figure}
\begin{itemize}
    \item With $C = 0.1$ (low regularization), the model prioritizes a wider margin at the potential cost of misclassifications.
    \item With $C = 1.0$ (balanced), we achieve a good trade-off between margin width and classification accuracy.
    \item With $C = 10.0$ (high regularization), the model focuses more on reducing training errors, potentially leading to narrower margins and possible overfitting.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Non-linear Data with Different Kernels}
We next evaluate our dual SVM implementation on non-linearly separable data using different kernels. Figure~\ref{fig:kernels} shows the performance of four different kernels on a synthetic dataset with circular decision boundaries.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/svm_kernels_comparison.png}
    \caption{Comparison of different kernels on non-linear data. From left to right: Linear, RBF, Polynomial, and Laplacian kernels.}
    \label{fig:kernels}
\end{figure}
\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.45\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{figures/kernel_convergence_comparison.png}
    \caption{Convergence behavior of different kernels during optimization.}
    \label{fig:kernel_convergence}
\end{wrapfigure}
As expected, the linear kernel performs the \enquote{worst} on the non-linear dataset, with a 99.67\% accuracy.
The RBF kernel with $\gamma=0.5$ perfectly captures the circular decision boundary with 100\% accuracy.
The polynomial kernel (degree 3) also performs well with 100\% accuracy but creates a more complex decision boundary.
The Laplacian kernel ($\gamma=0.1$) also achieves 100\% accuracy with a smooth decision boundary.
This demonstrates the flexibility of SVMs with different kernel functions, allowing them to adapt to various data distributions.

\subsection{Convergence Analysis of Different Kernels}

To examine kernel-specific optimization, we tracked dual SVM convergence on the non-linear dataset (Fig.~\ref{fig:kernel_convergence}).
RBF converged fastest (~80 iterations), linear took ~100 with mild oscillations, Laplacian needed ~200 stable steps, and polynomial exhibited the most instability, converging after ~200.
This aligns with theory that RBF is usually better-conditioned, while polynomial kernels may be numerically challenging.

\subsection{Breast Cancer Wisconsin Dataset}
Finally, we applied our SVM implementation to the Breast Cancer Wisconsin dataset, which contains 30 features computed from digitized images of fine needle aspirates (FNA) of breast mass, with binary labels indicating benign or malignant tumors.
\paragraph{Model Selection}
We evaluated multiple kernel types (Linear, RBF, Polynomial, Laplacian) with different $C$ values (0.1, 1.0, 10.0) on this dataset. Table~\ref{tab:cancer_results} shows the results.
\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{l|cc|cc|cc}
        \toprule
        & \multicolumn{2}{c|}{$C=0.1$} & \multicolumn{2}{c|}{$C=1.0$} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{$C=10.0$} \\
        \textbf{Kernel} & Train & Test & Train & Test & Train & Test \\
        \midrule
        Linear & 0.9824 & 0.9825 & 0.9868 & 0.9561 & 0.5077 & 0.5175 \\

        Polynomial (d=2) & - & - & 0.7780 & 0.7719 & - & - \\
        Laplacian ($\gamma=0.1$) & - & - & 0.9934 & 0.9649 & - & - \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Performance of different SVM configurations on the Breast Cancer Wisconsin dataset.}
    \label{tab:cancer_results}
\end{table}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{figures/breast_cancer_pca.png}
    \caption{Decision boundary visualization of the Breast Cancer dataset after PCA reduction. Red points represent benign tumors, blue points represent malignant tumors, and black circles indicate support vectors.}
    \label{fig:cancer_pca}
\end{figure}
The RBF kernel with $C=10.0$ achieved the best test accuracy of 98.25\%. Interestingly, the linear kernel with $C=0.1$ performed nearly as well, suggesting that the data might be largely linearly separable in the original feature space. The dramatic drop in performance for the linear kernel with $C=10.0$ indicates possible numerical issues or instability with high regularization values.
\paragraph{PCA Visualization:} We applied PCA to reduce the dataset to two dimensions and trained an RBF SVM on this reduced space (Fig.~\ref{fig:cancer_pca}). The first two components explain about 63\% of the variance, driven mainly by mean concavity, mean concave points, and worst concave points, which aid in distinguishing benign and malignant tumors.
\paragraph{Discussion:} Our experiments showed that primal and dual SVMs yield identical solutions for linearly separable data. The parameter $C$ strikes a balance between margin width and misclassifications, thus impacting generalization. Non-linear kernels greatly improve performance on data sets with complex decision boundaries. When applied to real data like Breast Cancer, the RBF kernel generally performs best, though the linear kernel also worked well. Convergence relies on careful tuning of learning rates, tolerance thresholds, and iterations. These findings illustrate SVMs’ flexibility and effectiveness, especially when paired with suitable kernels for non-linear tasks.
